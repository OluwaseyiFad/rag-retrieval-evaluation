An AtlasDesk security incident is an event in which the confidentiality,
integrity, or availability of customer data or services may be affected.
A support ticket about a suspected problem is not automatically a
security incident. An incident record contains an identifier, severity,
start time, affected service, current status, and incident commander.

Severity is based on customer impact. Severity One means a widespread
customer-facing outage or confirmed broad data exposure. Severity Two
means significant degradation affecting a subset of customers or a
critical feature. Severity Three covers limited impact with a documented
workaround. Severity Four covers an internal issue with no
customer-visible effect.

When an incident is declared, the incident commander assigns owners for
investigation, communications, and remediation. The communications owner
prepares customer updates. The remediation owner tracks technical
changes. The incident commander coordinates the work.

The first public update should state what is known, what is not known,
and what customers should do, if anything. It should not speculate about
the root cause. A later update may state a confirmed cause after the
investigation has established enough evidence. A status update and a
root-cause statement are therefore different things.

AtlasDesk keeps audit logs for administrative actions. An audit record
includes the actor, action, target object, timestamp, and result. Failed
administrative actions are also recorded. The standard audit-log
retention period is 180 days.

Conversation content follows a separate workspace retention policy.
Standard settings are 30, 90, or 365 days. Changing conversation
retention does not change the 180-day audit-log period. Retention begins
from the timestamp of the conversation’s final message, not from ticket
creation.

A legal hold temporarily suspends deletion for records covered by the
hold. If a conversation is past its normal retention period but is under
legal hold, it remains protected from deletion until the hold is
released. After release, normal retention processing can resume.

Deletion is asynchronous. When a conversation becomes eligible for
deletion, a background process evaluates it. A conversation may
therefore remain visible temporarily after becoming eligible. When a
customer asks why an old conversation is still visible, support should
check the eligibility timestamp, legal-hold status, and
deletion-processing status.

If an employee discovers suspicious access, they should preserve the
relevant identifiers and notify the security response group rather than
deleting or modifying records. Removing evidence can make timeline
reconstruction more difficult.

A service incident and a security incident can overlap but are not
identical. A service incident may involve downtime with no security
consequence. A security incident may involve unauthorized access without
an outage. When both dimensions are present, the operational and
security records are tracked separately and linked together.
